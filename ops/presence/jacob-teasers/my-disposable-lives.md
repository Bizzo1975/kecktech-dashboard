title: "My Disposable Lives"
summary: "An old drifter tells a young convict the stories of the seven men he has been — not knowing, at first, that his cellmate is the son he abandoned."
slug: my-disposable-lives
---

A novel in seven lives and one confession.

Two men share a cell. The old one talks to pass the dark: stories about a merchant seaman, a man who half-owned a drilling outfit in Texas, a stepfather who coached a small-town ball team, a deacon who ran a storefront church's charity, a man in the flophouses with no last name at all. Seven men, seven rises, seven stalls, seven falls. He offers each one to the young man as a lesson — work hard, be loyal, love anyway, tell the truth.

The young man keeps drawing different lessons than the ones he's handed. Sharper ones. Loyalty to the wrong thing, he points out, is just cowardice with company. Honesty deployed as a weapon is still violence. He does not know he is aiming these at the man on the other bunk. Neither, quite, does the old man — not at first.

They are all his lives, of course. He lived them under different names, and he tells six of them in the third person, the way you'd tell a story about someone else: *there was a man who.* The first life he ever lived is the one he tells last, and it is the only one he cannot manage in the third person.

I wanted a book with the warmth of a parable and the teeth of a real consequence, so I gave each life a virtue that fails it: the honesty life ends *because* he told the truth, at the wrong moment, on purpose. The pattern underneath all seven is the same one — every life ends in flight, and every flight is from the same thing. You can dispose of a life. You cannot dispose of yourself.

Set in America between 1958 and the mid-nineties, in the early, overconfident days of forensic DNA — a period when the science was new enough to be believed absolutely and new enough to be wrong about exactly one kind of match.
