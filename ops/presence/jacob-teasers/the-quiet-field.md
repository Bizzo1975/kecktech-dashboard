The field was never quiet. The receivers went quiet.

That is the premise I have been living inside for most of a year, and it is the reason this novel is thirty-six chapters instead of the twenty I planned. *The Quiet Field* is speculative fiction — every framework element in it is labeled speculative inside the story itself — and it asks one question with total seriousness: what if a genuine human capability existed, was carried by a real and ordinary practice, and was broken by a catastrophe far enough back that all we kept were the instructions?

Not a lost technology. A lost *sensitivity*. And the wreckage of it scattered across everything we've kept since: the acoustics of a megalithic chamber built to a resonance nobody in the crew could have named, a stamped disc read as a calendar for a century when it might have been a weather map for a condition we no longer have, a prayer whose timing is exactly right for reasons its keeper would never accept. She calls it the Shekhinah. She's right about what it is. She's wrong about why it's there.

Fourteen people assemble the case. Not a hero and a skeptic — fourteen, with fourteen honest positions, from the instrumentation man who stays hostile to the interpretation and stays anyway for the data, to the practitioner of forty-seven years who says flatly, *I don't believe in it, I do it.* Some of them are wrong about their own reasons. Not all of them stay. One of them keeps building a network she will not live long enough to use.

The activation happens once, in a granite cave on a farm in County Meath, and the person who goes into it is not the one the book has been training you to expect.

Structurally, this one is a bet. The flashbacks are not interruptions — they're the evidence itself, and you live inside each one before the researchers can explain it. You will be one step ahead of the science the whole way, and one step behind the grief.

Because the other side of the channel never stopped transmitting. What it sent, for most of twelve thousand years, was patience.
