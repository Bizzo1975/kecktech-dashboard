There is a theory I have been refining for most of my adult life, usually at a bar, usually against my will. It goes like this: every man sits somewhere on a scale of one to ten, and so does every woman, and nobody — *nobody* — is a zero. The number is not the problem. The lying about the number is the problem.

*Know Your Number* is the book that came out of that theory. It has laws. It has theorems. It has a table of field marks, the way a birding guide has field marks, because if you cannot identify what you are looking at, you cannot possibly plan for it. The Doormat apologizes to furniture and is secretly seething. The Archivist remembers what you said on a Tuesday in 2019, verbatim, with tone notes. None of this is an insult. It's a weather report.

The laws are the fun part. Everyone presents two or three points below their true number for the first ninety days, and the truth comes out under exactly three stressors: family, finances, or a flat tire. Two honest sevens will outlast two lying threes every time. And you cannot read your own number — only your exes can, and they round up.

What I did not plan on, when I started writing, was the other book underneath this one. The narrator spends the early chapters cheerfully scoring other people and assuring you that he's about a three. Watch the footnotes. The number climbs. By the chapter where he finally tells his own worst story, the whole cheerful apparatus turns around and looks at him — and, if I've done my job, at you.

The thesis fits in one line, and it took me eleven chapters of jokes to earn it: *you can't lower your number. You can only stop lying about it.*
